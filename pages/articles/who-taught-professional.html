Culture & Stories

Who Taught Us What Professional Looks Like?

Nobody has to explain the rules. You just know them—or at least, you think you do.

There are certain things you learn without anyone sitting you down to teach you. You learn how to greet an older person. You learn what to wear to an interview. You learn that you should arrive early. And, somewhere along the way, you learn what a professional person is supposed to look like.

The suit. The polished shoes. The pressed shirt. The quiet colours. The controlled hair. Nobody has to explain the rules. You just know them. Or at least, you think you do.

But look closely at the picture we have built in our heads. Whose face is in it? Whose hair? Whose features? Whose body?

For many Africans, this question becomes particularly uncomfortable when it comes to hair. Because there are hairstyles that can look perfectly respectable in one room and suddenly become “unprofessional” when worn by the wrong person.

A Black woman walks into a corporate office wearing a straight wig and nobody thinks twice. The same woman walks in the following week wearing her natural hair in a large afro and someone asks whether she has a meeting with clients. A man arrives with neatly maintained locs and somebody quietly wonders whether he is serious about his career. A woman wears braids to an interview and worries that the interviewer is looking at her hair rather than her qualifications.

None of these conversations need to happen out loud. That is the point. Standards can be powerful precisely because they don't announce themselves as standards. They simply feel like common sense.

Professionalism itself is not the problem. Of course people should be clean. Of course certain jobs have legitimate safety requirements. Of course a company can expect employees to dress appropriately for the work they do. A surgeon cannot have loose hair falling into an operating field. A construction worker may need protective equipment. A chef may need to secure their hair. These are practical requirements.

The uncomfortable part begins when practicality quietly turns into aesthetics. When “neat” starts meaning straight. When “presentable” starts meaning European. When “corporate” becomes another word for looking less African. That is a different conversation.

Colonialism did not simply take land and political power. It also disrupted ideas about what was considered respectable. European clothing, languages, manners and physical appearance were frequently associated with education, civilization and status under colonial rule. African ways of dressing and presenting oneself could be treated as evidence of backwardness. Hair was not immune from this.

And although colonial governments disappeared, some of the hierarchies they helped establish did not disappear with them. They moved. Into schools. Into workplaces. Into churches. Into families. Into our own understanding of ourselves.

This is why the modern corporate office can sometimes contain a very old idea: the closer you look to a European standard, the more professional you appear. Nobody needs to believe this consciously for it to have an effect. It can exist in hiring decisions, comments from managers, dress codes, what clients are expected to see, and what a young graduate decides to do with her hair the night before an interview.

She may not even be thinking about colonialism. She may simply be thinking: “I need to look professional.” And she already knows what that means.

There is something revealing about the language we use. “Wild.” “Unkempt.” “Untidy.” “Distracting.” “Too much.” These words can describe genuinely poor grooming. But they can also become attached to particular kinds of hair. An afro can be perfectly clean and carefully maintained and still be described as messy simply because it occupies more space. Locs can be meticulously cared for and still be perceived as unconventional. Braids can take hours to create and require considerable skill, yet the person wearing them may still be told that they need to look “more corporate.”

At some point, we have to ask whether the hair is actually untidy—or whether we are simply unfamiliar with it. Those are not the same thing.

There is another uncomfortable truth. Africans themselves participate in these standards. Sometimes enthusiastically. A mother tells her daughter that straight hair looks more respectable. A manager tells an employee to “do something” with their hair before an important presentation. A colleague compliments someone by saying they look “so professional” after straightening their hair. Nobody necessarily thinks they are enforcing colonialism. They may simply be repeating what they were taught.

This is how cultural ideas survive. Not through a secret meeting. Through ordinary conversations. One generation teaches another what looks respectable. And eventually nobody remembers who decided it.

But decolonizing beauty does not mean replacing one rule with another. It would be a strange kind of liberation if natural hair became another uniform. If wearing an afro became proof that you were sufficiently proud. If straight hair became evidence that someone had betrayed their culture. That would simply create another hierarchy.

The point is not that African hair is better. The point is that African hair should not automatically be treated as worse. There is a difference. A very important one.

A woman should be allowed to straighten her hair because she likes straight hair. She should also be allowed to wear her natural hair because she likes her natural hair. Neither choice should determine whether she is considered intelligent, disciplined or capable. Hair is not a résumé.

Corporate Africa is changing. Slowly. Young professionals are entering offices with hairstyles their parents might once have considered inappropriate for work. Natural hair is more visible. Braids are more visible. Locs are more visible. And companies are increasingly being forced to think about whether their grooming policies are actually necessary or simply inherited.

But the deeper change has to happen somewhere else. It has to happen in our definition of the word professional. Professional should describe how you work: whether you keep your commitments, know what you are doing, treat people with respect, can be trusted with responsibility, and show up prepared.

Hair cannot tell us any of those things. Neither can the texture of someone's skin, or the shape of their nose, or whether their features fit an old European image of refinement.

Perhaps that is what decolonizing beauty ultimately means. Not rejecting everything that came from Europe. Not pretending African cultures have never borrowed from anyone. Not declaring one hairstyle morally superior to another. It means becoming conscious of the standards we inherited. Looking at them. Questioning them. And deciding which ones still deserve to exist.

Because there is a strange thing about a rule that nobody remembers creating. It can feel like nature—until someone asks: Who taught us this?
