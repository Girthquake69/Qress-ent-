The Return to the Crown: Unlearning Texturism in the Modern World

Texturism teaches that some coils are more acceptable than others. Unlearning it is personal work and collective refusal.

Many of us learned early which textures were praised and which were policed — in school, in media, in the mirror. That hierarchy is texturism: discrimination within Black and African communities that ranks looser curls above tighter coils.

Where the hierarchy came from

Colonial and Eurocentric beauty standards did not only attack African features from outside; they filtered inward. Chemical straighteners and “professional” dress codes deepened the message that the natural crown was a problem to manage.

Workplace and public space

Crown-protection laws and natural hair movements push back against bias in hiring and schools. Visibility helps — but so does everyday courage: wearing locs, afros, twists and braids without apology.

Returning on purpose

Unlearning texturism means celebrating 4C as fully as 3A, refusing shame language, and teaching children that their hair is heritage. The return to the crown is not nostalgia. It is clarity.
