Who Really Owns a Hairstyle?

Same hair. Different verdict, depending on who’s wearing it and who’s watching.

Let's be honest — this conversation never used to happen. Braids were just braids. Threading was just threading. Nobody needed a hashtag to justify wearing what their grandmother taught them. Then the world discovered these styles, and suddenly Africa needed receipts to prove what was already ours.

That's the part that stings most. Not that people outside the continent wear cornrows or Bantu knots — styles travel, they always have. What stings is the amnesia. A style gets called “boxer braids” on one head and “ghetto” on another. It shows up on a magazine cover as innovation, while back home it got someone sent out of a job interview for being “unprofessional.” Same hair. Different verdict, depending on who's wearing it and who's watching.

Africa didn't invent hairstyling to be trendy. It invented it to communicate. Long before any of this was aesthetic, it was information. A woman's braid pattern could tell you her tribe, her marital status, her age, even her wealth. Enslaved Africans braided cassava routes and escape maps into cornrows while their captors saw only “hair.” That's not a fashion history — that's survival encoded in a style. Forgetting that isn't a small oversight. It's the erasure repeating itself in a new outfit.

So is it theft when someone outside the culture wears it? Not automatically. Culture was never meant to stay behind a fence. African styles cross borders within Africa too — a Yoruba pattern isn't a Zulu one isn't a Maasai one, and they've influenced each other for centuries without anyone needing permission. Movement isn't the crime.

The crime is silence. Wearing the style, taking the compliments, cashing the campaign check — and never once saying where it came from. That's not appreciation. That's laundering.

What should actually change is not the individual on the street wearing a style they admire. It is the institutions that decide which version of that style gets praised and which gets punished. The runways that “discover” what African women have done for generations. The offices — including African ones — still enforcing colonial beauty standards on natural styling. The media that waits for a non-African face before calling something beautiful.

Africa isn't asking for a monopoly on hairstyles. It's asking for the story to be told straight: this came from somewhere, from someone, from a lineage still fighting to wear it proudly without apology. Credit isn't a courtesy. It's the least the debt requires.
