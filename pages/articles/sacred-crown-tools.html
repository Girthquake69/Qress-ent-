The Sacred Crown: Iconic African Hair Tools Across Time

From ancient Nile Valley carved combs to modern ergonomic brushes — how the crown has been sculpted, protected and honoured across millennia of African hair artistry.

In traditional African societies, hair was never merely aesthetic. It was the highest point of the body, a conduit to the divine, and a visual language denoting royalty, age, marital status and spiritual readiness.

To style tightly coiled hair — with its unique density and helical memory — African artisans engineered purpose-built implements from hardwood, horn, bone, copper, silver and thread. These tools are not relics alone. They are a continuous legacy of hair sculpting science and heritage.

Here are eight iconic tools across time and tradition — from the Nile Valley to the Atlas Mountains, from the Horn of Africa to the modern natural-hair era.

Ancient · Nile Valley (Kemet & Kush) · c. 4000 BCE

Carved Wooden Afro Comb

Materials: Ebony wood, animal bone, ivory

Dating back to Predynastic Egypt and Nubia, long-toothed wooden combs were carved into high-tine profiles. Often topped with zoomorphic motifs or royal symbols, they served as grooming tools for dense coils and as ceremonial markers of nobility — sometimes placed as grave goods to signal eternal lineage.

Used with botanical oils to lift hair from the roots without disturbing intricate braid structures.

Traditional · Ghana (Ashanti / Akan)

Akan Dufe Comb

Materials: Sapele, mahogany, natural pigments

In Akan culture, the Dufe is a prized personal artifact — often given by husbands to wives or fathers to daughters. Carved from native hardwoods, these combs frequently carry Adinkra symbols such as Sankofa (return and fetch it) or Duafe (beauty, hygiene and feminine virtues).

Used to fluff threaded or braided crowns, adding volume while carrying symbolic protection.

Traditional Warrior Era · Kenya & Tanzania

Maasai Moran Hair Pins & Horn Scrapers

Materials: Polished horn, carved bone, red ochre, animal fat

Young Moran warriors are known for long red braids coloured with ground ochre and lipids. Bone pins and horn scrapers partition the scalp with geometric precision and smooth the ochre mixture down each strand.

Sectioning into fine strands, applying warm ochre paste, and smoothing for a uniform cylindrical finish.

Traditional Nomadic · Horn of Africa (Somalia / Kenya)

Somali Barkin (Wooden Headrest)

Materials: Hand-carved acacia or wild olive wood

The Barkin is an ergonomic carved rest used across pastoral Somali communities. Ceremonial coiffures could take days of work with gums, ghee and clay. Sleeping with the neck on a raised Barkin kept the crown elevated, protecting the structure overnight.

Elevates the base of the skull above the floor, reducing friction and preserving coiffures for extended wear.

Traditional & Modern · Kenya, Nigeria & West/East Africa

Hair Threading Threads (Nywele za Uzi)

Materials: Cotton thread, rubber thread, wool, copper needles

Hair threading — Kiko in Yoruba, Nywele za Uzi in Swahili — is a highly effective heatless stretching system. Taut thread wrapped around sectioned damp hair from root to tip elongates the coil pattern into geometric silhouettes without heat damage.

Hair is wound in spiral wraps and left to dry elongated into flexible, sculptable rods.

Traditional Berber · Morocco / North Africa

Amazigh Tazra & Braiding Adornments

Materials: Silver coins, coral beads, spun wool cords, amber

In the Atlas Mountains, Amazigh women wove complex crown braids reinforced with wool cording and hung with silver, amber and coral. These adornments supported dense hair weight and also displayed family wealth.

Braids anchored around heavy wool cords; silver and beads secure and celebrate each plait.

Traditional Pre-Colonial · Nigeria

Yoruba Irun Kiko Weaving Hooks

Materials: Carved hardwood, smooth bone, brass wire

Before plastic latch hooks, Yoruba hair sculptors crafted bone and hardwood hooks to loop and tie thread through base braid anchors — building elevated structures such as Onaba and Eko Bridge styles. The result is architectural: gravity-defying crowns woven from skill and patience.

Hooks latch thread beneath cornrows, raising strands into upright sculptural forms.

21st Century · Pan-African / Global Diaspora

Modern Flexi-Detangling Brushes

Materials: Flexible polymer, rubberised ergonomic handle

Unlike rigid brushes that snap coily shafts, flexi-brushes use independent, moving bristle rows on a flexible body. Designed with natural-hair needs in mind, the brush yields when it meets a knot — reducing tension and mechanical damage.

Best on wet, conditioned hair, working from ends toward roots as bristle arms adapt to coil density.

A continuous lineage

From ebony tines on the Nile to flexible polymers in today’s wash day, the through-line is the same: tools made for African hair on African terms. Not to “fix” the crown — to honour it.
